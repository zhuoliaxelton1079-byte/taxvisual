Your real marginal rate in retirement

Why the next dollar of a retiree’s income can cost far more than their tax bracket says — and what that means for a Roth conversion this year.

IssueWhat does the next dollar of income really cost?

The couple are considering converting part of a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA this year. The conversion is taxed as ordinary income. The question is not just which bracket they are in, but what each extra dollar actually costs once every rule that keys off their income is counted — and whether converting now beats paying tax on the same money later.

Short answerThe bracket understates the cost

LawFour rules key off the same income

Each rule is simple on its own. The cost comes from their overlap: a single extra dollar of IRA income can move all four at once.

1. Brackets and the standard deduction

Ordinary income above the standard deduction is taxed at 10% to 37%. For 2026 the standard deduction is $32,200 for a joint return and $16,100 for a single filer, plus $1,650 for each spouse aged 65 or older on a joint return ($2,050 for a single filer).

IRC §1(j), §63(c), §63(f); Rev. Proc. 2025-32

2. Taxation of Social Security benefits

Provisional income is other income plus tax-exempt interest plus half the benefits. Above $32,000 (joint) or $25,000 (single), each extra dollar makes 50¢ of benefits taxable; above $44,000 or $34,000, 85¢ — until 85% of the benefits are taxable. These thresholds have never been indexed for inflation. In the 85¢ zone one extra dollar of IRA income adds $1.85 of taxable income.

IRC §86

3. The senior deduction and its phase-out (2025–2028)

Each taxpayer aged 65 or older gets an extra deduction of up to $6,000, reduced by 6% of modified adjusted gross income above $150,000 (joint) or $75,000 (single). For a couple who both qualify, each dollar in the phase-out range removes 12¢ of deduction. It is scheduled to expire after 2028.

IRC §151(d)(5)(C), added by Pub. L. 119-21 §70103

4. Medicare premium surcharges (IRMAA)

Medicare Part B and Part D premiums rise in steps once modified adjusted gross income (adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest) passes $218,000 (joint) or $109,000 (single). The steps are cliffs: one dollar over a threshold raises the surcharge for the whole year, for each enrolled spouse. Premiums are set from income two years earlier, so 2026 income sets 2028 premiums; this memo uses the 2026 tiers as the best available estimate.

42 U.S.C. §1395r(i); 42 CFR §418.115; CMS 2026 premium announcement; SSA POMS HI 01101.020

ConclusionWhat to do this year

This memo answers one question — what converting costs now. A full recommendation also weighs the future rate more carefully (required distributions, a surviving spouse filing single, heirs’ brackets), where the tax on the conversion is paid from, and state tax.

AssumptionsWhat this memo leaves out

  • Federal tax only, for tax year 2026. No state income tax.
  • All other income is ordinary (pensions, IRA distributions, interest). Qualified dividends and capital gains, which stack differently, are not modelled.
  • Both spouses are 65 or older and enrolled in Medicare Parts B and D; the single filer likewise.
  • Standard deduction, no itemizing. No credits, and no net investment income tax (IRA income is not investment income, though it raises the income that tax keys off).
  • Medicare surcharges use the 2026 tiers for income that will be measured for 2028 premiums.
